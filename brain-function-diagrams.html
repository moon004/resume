TEDxUniversity of Tartu · 2 June 2026 · Kristina Kallas — Estonia's Minister of Education and Research

Above the Waterline

When AI knows everything, what should humans learn? Five diagrams from a talk about a brain under pressure — and the small set of skills still worth the electricity. Each one comes with a plain-language explainer, no jargon left standing.

§1

The waterline is rising through the ladder

Kallas opens by saying she won't talk about AI at all. She'll talk about humans — and about which floors of human thinking a machine now reaches.

ELI18

Educators sort thinking into six stacked levels, called Bloom's taxonomy. Bottom three: remember a fact, understand it well enough to explain, apply the standard method to a standard problem. Top three: analyze (take an idea apart and see how it works), evaluate (judge whether it's any good, and say why), create (build something that didn't exist before). School — most school, in most countries — grades you overwhelmingly on the bottom three, because they're easy to test.

Kallas's point: a language model now does the bottom three on demand — it retrieves, summarizes, calculates, and drafts faster than you ever will. That's the waterline in the picture, and it's rising. Anything below it can be faked with a prompt, which means a diploma built below it stops certifying anything. This isn't the end of learning; it's the same shove the printing press gave us. Books didn't make reading pointless — they made literacy for everyone mandatory. AI doesn't make thinking pointless — it makes the top of the ladder the new literacy.

§2

Your brain runs two billing plans

The talk's core model of how the brain works: a cheap autopilot mode it prefers, and an expensive deliberate mode it avoids — exactly the mode AI now lets you skip.

ELI18

Your brain is about 2% of your body weight and burns roughly 20% of your energy, so evolution made it a ruthless accountant. Whatever can run on autopilot — recognizing, recalling, repeating a practiced routine — runs there, because it's nearly free. The other mode, deliberate thought, is what you feel when you hold three things in your head at once, weigh them against each other, and assemble something new. It's slow, it's expensive, and it genuinely feels bad, the way squats feel bad. That feeling is not a malfunction. It's the price tag.

Kallas's warning is about the fork in the picture: given any excuse, your brain takes the cheap road. AI is the ultimate excuse — a machine that will do the expensive mode's output at cheap-mode cost. Say yes every time and you get a life lived entirely on autopilot: fluent, productive-looking, and slowly emptying. Her proposal is the opposite: treat deliberate mode like a muscle and deliberately train it — her phrase is holding people "in the flow of higher cognitive processes" — precisely because the machine has made avoiding it effortless.

Honesty footnote: "two modes" is a working model (psychologists call it dual-process thinking), not literal anatomy — real brains run overlapping networks, and how hard a task feels depends on what you already know. The energy asymmetry and the default-to-cheap bias, though, are solid science.

§3

Struggle is the ink the brain writes with

Why the cheap road has a hidden cost: learning is physical, and only effort triggers the write. This is the mechanism behind Kallas's central design rule — thinking with AI versus letting AI think instead.

ELI18

Learning isn't a download; it's construction work. When you strain at a problem — genuinely reach for an answer, get it partly wrong, see why, and try again — the neurons involved fire together, and the connections between them physically strengthen. Do laps of that loop, spaced over days, and you've built a circuit: knowledge you can use at autopilot prices forever after. Skip the strain and there's nothing for the brain to build with. The awkward, effortful reaching is the trigger. Scientists literally call it desirable difficulty.

Which explains the trap on the right of the picture. Ask AI, receive a polished answer, nod along — it feels like learning, because the material passed before your eyes and made sense. But you never attempted, never missed, never adjusted, so nothing was written. You rented the understanding; you don't own it. Same tool, held differently, does the opposite: you draft first, then make the AI attack your reasoning, and the loop stays intact with a sparring partner inside it. That's the whole distinction Kallas hangs the future of education on — thinking with AI versus letting AI think instead. The machine is identical in both. What differs is who does the reps.

§4

The Socratic flip: Estonia rewires the tutor

Estonia's national experiment, AI Leap (TI-Hüpe): the same chatbots every student already uses — licensed, then flipped so the questions point the other way.

ELI18

Estonia's bet — the program is called AI Leap, TI-Hüpe in Estonian — is that you don't fight the machine, you re-aim it. They licensed custom school versions of ChatGPT and Gemini with one behavioral change: the bot won't hand over the finished essay. Ask it, and it asks you — what's your claim? what's your evidence? what would someone who disagrees say? It's the 2,400-year-old Socratic method with infinite patience and no office hours.

Look at what that flip does to the diagram. In the left circuit you fire one question and receive one product; the loop from §3 runs entirely inside the machine, so the machine gets the reps. In the right circuit you produce and the machine supplies the friction — exactly the miss-feedback-adjust lap your neurons need to write anything down. Teachers aren't cut out; they're retrained as the orchestrator who decides when the sparring is productive and when a student needs a human. The AI handles the routine rungs below the waterline so the classroom's scarce human hours move above it.

§5

So what should we focus on?

The talk's answer, in one line: train to stay in the flow of higher cognitive processes — and aim the training at five capacities machines can't certify for you.

ELI18

Flow is the psychologist's name for the state where a task is pitched just past your current ability — you're fully absorbed, working at the edge, and (recall §3) the edge is exactly where the brain writes. Pitch the task below you and you slide into autopilot: comfortable, cheap, nothing stored. Pitch it too far above and you shut down. The channel between them is narrow, and Kallas's "evolutionary leap" line is literally a national plan to keep as many people as possible inside it — using AI not to remove the challenge but to keep re-raising it as your reach grows.

And the five stations? They're her answer to the title question. When the machine knows everything, what it still can't be for you is the one who decides what matters, smells a weak answer, connects the dots across worlds, carries the ethical call, and signs their name to something new:

  1. 1

    Judgment — decide what matters

    Infinite answers make the scarce skill choosing the question. Prioritizing, framing, knowing what to ignore.

    train it: before any AI session, write the decision you're actually trying to make — then grade the session against it.

  2. 2

    Detection — recognize when a fluent answer is weak

    Models produce confident prose at any quality level. Fluency stopped being evidence; you need a nose for the smooth-but-hollow.

    train it: fact-check one polished AI answer per day in a domain you know. Find the soft spot before you're told where it is.

  3. 3

    Transfer — connect knowledge across contexts

    Taking a structure from one world into another — biology into economics, chess into negotiation — is analysis and creation fused, and it's where new ideas come from.

    train it: for each big idea you learn, force one application in a field it didn't come from. Write it down; most will be bad; the reps count anyway.

  4. 4

    Ethics — make the judgment calls

    A model can list the considerations; it can't be accountable. Deciding what should happen — and owning the consequences — stays human.

    train it: argue the side you disagree with, in writing, until it's the strongest version of itself. Then decide, and say why out loud.

  5. 5

    Creation — make work you can defend

    Not the first plausible draft — the machine owns that. Work where you chose every load-bearing part and can survive cross-examination on it.

    train it: ship things with your name on them, then invite the cross-examination (a Socratic bot does fine). Defending choices is a rep, too.

"Our chance for an evolutionary leap is to consciously train as many people as possible to be constantly in the flow of higher cognitive processes."
— Kristina Kallas, TEDxUniversity of Tartu, June 2026 (as reported in published accounts of the talk)
