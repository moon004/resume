TEDxUniversity of Tartu · 2 June 2026 · Kristina Kallas — Estonia's Minister of Education and Research

Above the Waterline

When AI knows everything, what should humans learn? Five diagrams from a talk about a brain under pressure — and the small set of skills still worth the electricity. Each one comes with a plain-language explainer, no jargon left standing.

§1

The waterline is rising through the ladder

Kallas opens by saying she won't talk about AI at all — she'll talk about humans. The whole thing began, she admits, with an adviser repeating "we need to do something about AI" and neither of them knowing what.

ELI18

The talk starts as a confession. Kallas became Estonia's education minister in April 2023 — five months after ChatGPT launched — and her adviser kept appearing with the same two sentences: we need to do something about AI, and I'm not sure what. So they spent a year with neuroscientists, cognitive scientists, and tech companies, and came back with a reframe: this is not a technology question, it's a learning question — what happens to the human brain's capacity to learn when a machine this capable is always in the room. One number sharpened it: a 2017 OECD skills survey found two-thirds of the workforce already scored below the computers of that time on literacy, numeracy, and digital skills. Which lets her ask the taboo question out loud: should humans just give up learning?

Her answer starts on the ladder above — Bloom's taxonomy, the standard six-level map of thinking. For 200 years, school has lived on the bottom rungs — memorize the fact ("WWII began in 1939"), understand it, apply the standard method — because those are easy to test; the top rungs mostly waited for the PhD. The problem: by her account AI now runs everything up through analysis on demand, and its only weak rungs are ethical evaluation and genuine creation — it can assemble the statistically probable, "but the human creation is completely something else." Humanity has been shoved like this before: the printing press didn't make reading pointless, it made literacy universal and non-negotiable. Same shove, several floors up. AI doesn't make thinking pointless — it makes the top of the ladder the new literacy.

§2

Your brain runs two billing plans

The talk's core model of how the brain works: a cheap autopilot mode it prefers, and an expensive deliberate mode it avoids — exactly the mode AI now lets you skip.

ELI18

Your brain is your body's hungriest organ — the talk leans its whole argument on that fact — so evolution made it a ruthless accountant. Whatever can run on autopilot runs there, because autopilot is nearly free: riding a bicycle, skiing, reciting "1939" when someone asks about the war — learned once, replayed forever, never re-examined. Her sharpest example: stereotypes live there too. "Italians are loud" is a cached conclusion your brain replays so it doesn't have to spend energy relearning the actual person in front of you. Deliberate mode is the opposite discipline — constant relearning, holding pieces in mind, weighing them, building something new — and it burns. Her benchmark: study for a brutal exam and "you will be dead next day, physically." Harder, she says, than running a marathon — because the marathon mostly runs on autopilot.

Kallas's warning is about the fork in the picture: given any excuse, your brain takes the cheap road. AI is the ultimate excuse — a machine that will do the expensive mode's output at cheap-mode cost. Her verdict on that lifestyle is blunt: in the AI era, staying parked in the lower mode "is not an option any longer." The project instead is to treat deliberate mode like a muscle and train it — spend "much more awake hours" in higher-order thinking — precisely because the machine has made avoiding it effortless.

Honesty footnote: "two modes" is a working model (psychologists call it dual-process thinking), not literal anatomy — real brains run overlapping networks, and how hard a task feels depends on what you already know. The energy asymmetry is real, though: the standard figure behind her point is a brain at ~2% of body mass burning ~20% of resting energy. The default-to-cheap bias is solid science.

§3

Struggle is the ink the brain writes with

Why the cheap road has a hidden cost: learning is physical, and only effort triggers the write. This is the mechanism behind the fear that launched Estonia's program — in her words, "brain rot and cognitive offloading."

ELI18

Learning isn't a download; it's construction work. When you strain at a problem — genuinely reach for an answer, get it partly wrong, see why, and try again — the neurons involved fire together, and the connections between them physically strengthen. Do laps of that loop, spaced over days, and you've built a circuit: knowledge you can use at autopilot prices forever after. Skip the strain and there's nothing for the brain to build with. The awkward, effortful reaching is the trigger. Scientists literally call it desirable difficulty.

Which explains the trap on the right of the picture. Ask AI, receive a polished answer, nod along — it feels like learning, because the material passed before your eyes and made sense. But you never attempted, never missed, never adjusted, so nothing was written. You rented the understanding; you don't own it. Kallas's team named that endpoint precisely — "brain rot and cognitive offloading" — and doing nothing about it was what they feared most. But notice the twist in her fix: the cure is not less AI. She argues the machine is exactly "the one that pushes us" up the ladder — "without AI, we kind of leap back" to memorize-understand-apply. Same tool on both sides of the picture. What differs is who does the reps — and that's the flip Estonia engineered next.

§4

The Socratic flip: Estonia rewires the tutor

Estonia's national experiment, AI Leap (TI-Hüpe), live since February 2025: not a general-purpose chatbot but a Socratic version of ChatGPT built with OpenAI — flipped so the questions point at the student.

ELI18

Estonia's bet — the program is called AI Leap, TI-Hüpe in Estonian — is that you don't fight the machine, you re-aim it. The strategy was written by the end of 2024; the pilot went live in February 2025 with 10th and 11th graders, and vocational students join in September 2026. It's a public-private partnership — half the budget from companies, half from the ministry — and it's deliberately run by an independent foundation, because, Kallas admits, a ministry "is not the best place" for this kind of innovation. The tool was co-developed with OpenAI, and she's precise about what it is: "It's not a general-purpose ChatGPT… it's actually a Socratic version of ChatGPT for schools." It won't hand over the essay. It asks you — what's your claim? what's your evidence? what would someone who disagrees say? The 2,400-year-old method, now with infinite patience and no office hours.

Look at what that flip does to the diagram. In the left circuit you fire one question and receive one product; the loop from §3 runs entirely inside the machine, so the machine gets the reps. In the right circuit you produce and the machine supplies the friction — exactly the miss-feedback-adjust lap your neurons need to write anything down. That's why "the absolute focus" of the program is teachers, retrained as the orchestrators who decide when the sparring is productive and when a student needs a human. And she is candid about the frontier: there's scientific monitoring, but "we are really observing and learning as we go" — Estonia is "the first and so far the only country" to hand students a Socratic tutor, and everyone is watching. The standard comes from Estonia's president at the launch: it's not about using AI the most; it's about how, and what for. "Let's use it the wisest way."

§5

So what should we focus on?

The talk's answer, in one line: consciously train as many people as possible to stay in the flow of higher cognitive processes — starting around age 12, not at the PhD.

ELI18

Flow is the psychologist's name for the state where a task is pitched just past your current ability — you're fully absorbed, working at the edge, and (recall §3) the edge is exactly where the brain writes. Pitch the task below you and you slide into autopilot: comfortable, cheap, nothing stored. Pitch it too far above and you shut down. The channel between them is narrow, and Kallas's "evolutionary leap" line is literally a national plan to keep as many people as possible inside it — using AI not to remove the challenge but to keep re-raising it as your reach grows.

And the five stations are the talk's own vocabulary, not mine. The evolutionary pressure, she says, is to "think faster, more systematically and much more creatively"; the Socratic tutor exists to push "more analytical thinking, more systematic thinking and more creative thinking and critical thinking"; and the training has to reach everyone — in Estonia's curriculum ambition from 7th grade, age 12 or 13, instead of waiting for the PhD. Two of the stations — ethical evaluation and creation — are the rungs she says AI is still "not very good" at. The other three are how you climb to them:

  1. 1

    Analysis — take it apart

    Her example: not when did the war start, but why 1939 — the actors, their relationships, the motives behind their actions. The fact as a system of causes, not a flashcard.

    train it: never stop at the what. For anything you learn, write the why-chain: three causes, and a cause behind each cause.

  2. 2

    Systematic thinking — see the whole board

    Holding many moving parts and their relationships in view at once — the mode her exam story burns all that energy on, and half of "think faster, more systematically."

    train it: turn whatever you're studying into a parts-and-arrows diagram from memory, then check what you missed. (This page is the format.)

  3. 3

    Critical thinking — interrogate the fluent answer

    The Socratic tutor's whole job is to do this to you — evidence? counter-case? — until you reflexively do it to every polished paragraph, machine-made included.

    train it: fact-check one confident AI answer per day in a domain you know. Find the soft spot before you're told where it is.

  4. 4

    Ethical evaluation — make the call

    One of the two rungs she flags as still human: AI is "not very good" at ethical evaluation. A model can list considerations; it can't be accountable for the judgment.

    train it: argue the side you disagree with, in writing, until it's the strongest version of itself. Then decide, and say why out loud.

  5. 5

    Creation — make the genuinely new

    The other human rung. AI "creates" out of statistical probability; "the human creation is completely something else. Human brain is in this sense still unique."

    train it: ship work with your name on it, then invite the cross-examination — a Socratic bot does fine. Defending choices is a rep, too.

"We have to consciously train humans — as many people as possible — to be constantly in the flow of this higher cognitive thinking processes. And this is a human brain training."
— Kristina Kallas, TEDxUniversity of Tartu, June 2026 — verbatim from the transcript
